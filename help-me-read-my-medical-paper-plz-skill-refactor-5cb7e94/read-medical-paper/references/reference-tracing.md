# Reference-First Knowledge Building And Citation Tracing

## Purpose

Build the minimum reliable background knowledge needed before judging a paper's novelty, mechanism, methods, and broader meaning.

## When To Use

Use available reference material for every whole-paper analysis and when the user asks about a mechanism or cited claim. Verify externally when tools permit; otherwise label the ledger provisional and state which primary findings or statuses could not be checked.

## Core Checklist

### 1. Select Load-Bearing References

Do not read every citation. Select the few sources that carry the paper's logic:

- Define the central mechanism or disease context.
- Introduce a key method, model, reagent, dataset, or statistical approach.
- Provide the closest prior therapeutic or experimental proof.
- Provide the clinical guideline or consensus context.
- Contradict, complicate, or limit the current paper.
- Are necessary background for a figure, pathway, biomarker, or endpoint.

Prefer primary studies for factual claims. Use reviews or consensus papers to orient terminology and locate primary evidence.

### 2. Verify And Read

When lookup is available, verify title, authors, journal, year, DOI/PMID, publication status, corrections, and retractions. Read enough of the primary source to determine what it directly showed; do not rely on the current paper's one-line characterization.

Trace in two directions:

- Backward tracing: inspect references cited by the paper.
- Forward tracing: if web/search tools are available, look for later papers that cite the paper or key references.

If lookup tools are available, choose sources by need:

- Biomedical record or abstract: PubMed.
- Biomedical full text: PMC when available.
- DOI and journal metadata: Crossref.
- Citation graph or later citing papers: Semantic Scholar or OpenAlex.
- Open-access PDF: Unpaywall, PMC, or CORE.
- Preprints: bioRxiv, medRxiv, or arXiv; check whether a journal version exists.

For surprising, famous, or clinical claims, also check for retraction notices, expressions of concern, failed replications, and contradictory later evidence.

### 3. Build The Reference-Derived Knowledge Ledger

| Reference and role | What it directly established | How the current paper uses it | Inherited versus new | Transfer risk, contradiction, or dependence | Verification/status |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Foundational mechanism | Exact prior result | Background or premise | What is inherited; what remains for this paper | Model, population, assay, or same-team limitation | DOI/PMID/status checked |

After the ledger, synthesize four statements:

- **Established before this paper**
- **Inherited or reused by this paper**
- **Newly demonstrated in this paper**
- **Still unsupported or contradicted**

Treat a same-team companion paper, reused public dataset, inherited animal model, or repeated assay as dependent evidence, not independent replication.

### 4. Return To The Current Paper

Use the knowledge base to judge novelty and transferability, but use the current paper's figures, methods, and supplements—not its references—as evidence for its own results. State where a cited source supports a narrower claim than the current paper makes.

### 5. Stop Efficiently

Stop tracing when the central concepts, inherited methods/models/data, closest prior evidence, and one meaningful limiting or contradictory source are covered. Add more only if they can change interpretation.

## Common Pitfalls

- Chasing every citation instead of the few that carry the argument.
- Starting the whole-paper interpretation before learning what the paper inherits.
- Trusting a citation because it is famous.
- Treating review articles as primary evidence.
- Treating a companion paper or reused dataset as independent confirmation.
- Calling an inherited method, model, or dataset a new contribution.
- Forgetting to check whether a method reference proves validity in this context.
- Inventing a DOI, PMID, author list, citation count, or retraction status from memory.

## Minimal Example

Synthetic mechanism claim: if a paper says "Pathway A activates Cell State B," first trace the original pathway experiment, the source of the Cell State B signature, any inherited animal model, and one contradictory source. Then state which pieces were already known and which causal step the current paper actually adds.
